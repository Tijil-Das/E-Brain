If he was going to ask this man and his crew to sail into uncharted northern waters with dangerous enemies already on their wake, Captain Reed deserved the whole truth--and by the time the autumn sun set over New Bedford Harbor that afternoon, the expedition to the First Anchorage had found its commander.

By the end of the first week of May, the winter ice in the North Atlantic had finally begun to fracture and drift southward in vast, glittering floes, opening a navigable channel past the southern tip of Greenland toward the volcanic silhouette of Jan Mayen Island.

Arthur stood beside the helm on the quarterdeck of the *Endurance*, wrapped in a heavy oilskin coat and sheepskin-lined cap, watching the gray swells roll endlessly beneath the schooner's bow. Six months had passed since that tense morning on Pier 9 in New Bedford--six months of meticulous preparation that had transformed an aging cartographer into the organizer of a true polar expedition.

Once Captain Tobias Reed had heard the full account of Julian's discovery and examined the photographic plates Dr. Finch had prepared in Cambridge, the veteran seaman had not flinched. Instead, Reed had thrown himself into outfitting the *Endurance* with quiet, methodical brilliance. They had reinforced the forward hull ribs with seasoned white oak, installed a new high-pressure boiler for the auxiliary steam engine, packed the hold with six months of provisions, scientific sounding gear, portable arc lamps, and heavy core-drilling equipment, and hand-picked a crew of ten seasoned sailors from Nova Scotia and Maine who knew how to keep their mouths shut in waterfront taverns.

To throw Gareth Sterling and his consortium off their scent, Reed had filed clearance papers in New Bedford for a routine meteorological and sealing voyage to Disko Bay on the western coast of Greenland. They had sailed north through the Cabot Strait in mid-April, put in briefly at St. John's in Newfoundland to top off their coal bunkers, and then altered course sharply eastward in the dense fog off Cape Farewell, running dark and silent across the open Denmark Strait toward the Greenland Sea.

For three weeks they had seen neither sail nor smoke on the horizon. Now, on the morning of May 11, the barometer had dropped steadily while a strange, eerie stillness settled over the ocean. The wind had died to a whisper, leaving the gray sea rolling in long, glassy swells beneath a sky the color of tarnished pewter.

We're crossing the sixty-eighth parallel now, Mr. Pendelton, Captain Reed said, stepping out of the wheelhouse with a brass sextant tucked under his arm. Just passed twenty miles west of Jan Mayen's northern cape--though you wouldn't know it through this mist. Funny thing about the compass, though. Mr. Higgins at the wheel says the binnacle needle has been swinging three points east and west for the last half hour, even with the compensating magnets adjusted.

Arthur reached into his oilskin coat and drew out the brass astrolabe, holding it flat in his gloved palm. Just as Dr. Finch had predicted in Cambridge, the central silver-inlaid star had shifted slightly within its bezel, and when Arthur placed a small pocket compass beside the instrument, the needle snapped instantly toward the north-northwest, trembling with a faint, steady vibration.

Julian's bearings were exact, Arthur said quietly, looking out over the bow into the pale wall of sea fog. Keep her on this magnetic heading at half steam, Captain. According to the 1911 sounding log, the underwater basalt escarpment rises abruptly from twelve hundred fathoms within the next four miles.

Reed gave the order down the speaking tube to the engine room, and the steady thump of the auxiliary steam pistons slowed to a rhythmic, cautious pulse. Two crewmen stationed themselves in the bow chains with heavy lead lines, calling out the depth every two minutes as the schooner glided silently through the mist.

No bottom at two hundred fathoms! the leadsman sang out, his voice carrying clearly across the still water.

Minutes dragged by in tense silence. Arthur stood at the bow rail beside the first mate, squinting into the milky fog ahead, while the brass astrolabe grew noticeably warm against his palm through the wool of his glove.

Bottom at sixty fathoms--hard basalt! the leadsman shouted suddenly, hauling his line in rapidly. Forty fathoms! Twenty-five fathoms!

Hard astern! Reed bellowed from the quarterdeck, spinning the engine-room telegraph himself.

The *Endurance* shuddered as her propeller reversed, churning the glassy gray water into white foam, and glided to a gentle halt less than a cable's length from a dark, towering wall of columnar basalt that loomed out of the mist like the battlements of a sunken fortress.

Arthur stared upward in awe. Rising nearly eighty feet sheer out of the freezing sea was a crescent of jagged black volcanic rock, crowned with patches of ancient blue glacial ice. Directly ahead, where two massive pillars of basalt leaned inward toward one another, lay a narrow, deep-water channel no wider than sixty feet, leading into a sheltered, circular lagoon entirely hidden from the open ocean.

The First Anchorage, Arthur breathed, unfolding Julian's vellum chart and comparing the twin basalt pillars against the ink sketch penned in 1911. It is all here--the exact hexagonal basin, the sheltered entrance, everything the 1504 pilot described.

With two crewmen stationed at the bow with long wooden fenders and the steam engine turning over at its lowest speed, Captain Reed guided the *Endurance* inch by inch through the narrow basalt gap. As the schooner cleared the entrance pillars and entered the circular basin beyond, the heavy sea mist thinned abruptly, revealing a sight that brought every man on deck to a stunned, breathless standstill.

The basin was a near-perfect hexagon roughly four hundred yards across, surrounded on all sides by sheer cliffs of dark basalt that shielded the water from the ocean swells. At the northern end of the lagoon, resting on a broad shelf of black stone ten feet above the waterline, stood a circular tower built of seamless, dark gray masonry blocks fitted together without a trace of mortar. The tower was perhaps sixty feet high, tapering slightly toward a flat parapet, and from an open lantern gallery at its summit rose a steady, smokeless column of pale blue-white light--cold, silent, and brilliant enough to cast sharp shadows across the dark water even in the gray Arctic daylight.

The stone tower that burns without fuel, Captain Reed muttered, slowly removing his peaked cap as he stared across the water. God above, Mr. Pendelton--your uncle wasn't mad after all.

Within twenty minutes, the *Endurance* had dropped anchor in fifteen fathoms of clear, black water fifty yards off the stone shelf, and Arthur, Captain Reed, and two armed seamen rowed ashore in the ship's dory, carrying lanterns, measuring instruments, and a heavy pry bar.

When Arthur stepped out of the dory onto the smooth, wet basalt landing, he felt a faint, steady hum vibrating up through the soles of his boots--the same subtle resonance he had felt in the brass astrolabe. Broad steps cut directly into the living rock led up toward the base of the tower, where a tall, arched portal stood recessed behind two fluted stone columns engraved with the exact same geometric star pattern inlaid into the central key of the astrolabe.

Arthur approached the portal slowly, heart hammering against his ribs. Set into the center of the seamless dark metal door at waist height was a circular hollow surrounded by three raised bronze pins.

Without hesitation, Arthur drew the brass astrolabe from his coat, pressed the release catch, and removed the assembled silver-inlaid disk from its center. Holding his breath, he placed the disk into the circular recess on the door.

The three internal pins engaged with a deep, resonant chime that echoed across the stone basin. The geometric star on the disk flared briefly with the same pale blue luminescence burning atop the tower, and with a low, effortless grind of counterweights concealed deep within the basalt cliff, the massive metal door slid smoothly sideways into the wall.
