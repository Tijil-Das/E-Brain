 he could even raise his arm, a heavy blow struck him squarely across the back of the shoulder, driving him forward onto his knees on the rough wooden planks.

Arthur gasped in pain, clutching instinctively at the strap of his leather satchel as two men stepped out from behind the timber stacks. One was broad-shouldered and thick-necked, wearing a dockworker's wool cap pulled low over his brow; the other was tall, lean, and dressed in the same tailored charcoal overcoat Arthur had seen in the basement corridor at Harvard the afternoon before.

Gareth Sterling looked down at Arthur with cold, detached composure, adjusting the cuffs of his leather gloves as he spoke. Good morning, Mr. Pendelton. You led us on a rather brisk chase from Cambridge, I must admit. Checking the local railway schedules out of Providence was a tedious business, though New Bedford was the obvious choice once we considered your uncle's fondness for old whaling ports.

Arthur struggled to push himself up onto one knee, his right shoulder throbbing fiercely, while his left hand remained locked tightly around the brass buckle of his satchel. Who are you? he demanded, glancing quickly toward the schooner at the end of the pier, which was hidden from view by the stacked timber. What do you want with my uncle's papers?

We represent an international consortium that has spent fifteen years assembling the historical and scientific records of the northern meridian, Sterling replied smoothly, gesturing slightly with his chin to the broad-shouldered man beside him. Your uncle stumbled across a fragment of something far beyond his comprehension—and far beyond yours. Give me the satchel, Mr. Pendelton, and we will leave you here on the pier with nothing worse than a bruised shoulder. Refuse, and I am afraid my associate will ensure you never board that gray schooner you were just inspecting.

The thick-necked man stepped forward, reaching down with one massive hand to wrench the leather satchel from Arthur's grasp. Arthur pulled back with all his strength, twisting sideways so that the strap held fast across his chest, while his free hand groped desperately along the rough planks of the pier—and closed around a heavy, iron-tipped wooden cant hook left lying beside the timber stack by the yard workers.

As the assailant leaned down to strike him again, Arthur swung the heavy wooden shaft upward with both hands, catching the man squarely across the shin. With a sharp curse, the man stumbled backward against the timber stack, momentarily losing his balance.

Before Sterling could draw the revolver already visible beneath the lapel of his charcoal overcoat, a thunderous voice roared from the far end of the timber aisle: Drop that iron right now, or I'll put a load of buckshot through the both of you!

Captain Tobias Reed stood twenty paces away near the edge of the pier, a double-barreled brass-mounted shotgun leveled squarely at Sterling's chest, flanked by two burly deckhands carrying heavy oak belaying pins.

Sterling froze, his hand hovering near his coat lapel, his pale eyes darting between Reed's steady shotgun barrels and the open street at the head of the pier. Realizing the advantage had vanished in an instant, Sterling gave a curt, almost imperceptible nod to his bruised companion.

This is merely a delay, Mr. Pendelton, Sterling said coldly, backing slowly toward the street entrance of the pier. The Greenland Sea is a very large, very lonely place, and you will not be the only vessel heading north when the ice breaks in May.

Within seconds, both men had disappeared around the corner of the brick sail loft onto Water Street. Captain Reed lowered the shotgun slowly, walked over to where Arthur was still kneeling beside the timber stack, and extended a strong hand to help him to his feet.

Well now, Mr. Pendelton, Reed said grimly, eyeing the bruised shoulder of Arthur's tweed coat and the leather satchel clutched tightly against his ribs. Looks like there's a bit more to this mineral survey of yours than rocks and cold water. Come back aboard the *Endurance* and let's have a glass of brandy—and then you can tell me the real reason those fellows want what's inside that bag.

Arthur stood swaying slightly on the wooden planks, taking a deep, steady breath of the cold salt air. Looking into Captain Reed's shrewd, unflinching eyes, Arthur knew that half-truths would no longer serve. If he was going to ask this man and his crew to sail into uncharted northern waters with dangerous enemies already on their wake, Captain Reed deserved the whole truth—and by the time the autumn sun set over New Bedford Harbor that afternoon, the expedition to the First Anchorage had found its commander.

By the end of the first week of May, the winter ice in the North Atlantic had finally begun to fracture and drift southward in vast, glittering floes, opening a navigable channel past the southern tip of Greenland toward the volcanic silhouette of Jan Mayen Island.

Arthur stood beside the helm on the quarterdeck of the *Endurance*, wrapped in a heavy oilskin coat and sheepskin-lined cap, watching the gray swells roll endlessly beneath the schooner's bow. Six months had passed since that tense morning on Pier 9 in New Bedford—six months of meticulous preparation that had transformed an aging cartographer into the organizer of a true polar expedition.

Once Captain Tobias Reed had heard the full account of Julian's discovery and examined the photographic plates Dr. Finch had prepared in Cambridge, the veteran seaman had not flinched. Instead, Reed had thrown himself into outfitting the *Endurance* with quiet, methodical brilliance. They had reinforced the forward hull ribs with seasoned white oak, installed a new high-pressure boiler for the auxiliary steam engine, packed the hold with six months of provisions, scientific sounding gear, portable arc lamps, and heavy core-drilling equipment, and hand-picked a crew of ten seasoned sailors from Nova Scotia and Maine who knew how to keep their mouths shut in waterfront taverns.

To throw Gareth Sterling and his consortium off their scent, Reed had filed clearance papers in New Bedford for a routine meteorological and sealing voyage to Disko Bay on the western coast of Greenland. They had sailed north through the Cabot Strait in mid-April, put in briefly at St. John's in Newfoundland to top off their coal bunkers, and then altered course sharply eastward in the dense fog off Cape Farewell, running dark and silent across the open Denmark Strait toward the Greenland Sea.

For three weeks they had seen neither sail nor smoke on the horizon. Now, on the morning of May 11, the barometer had dropped steadily while a strange, eerie stillness settled over the ocean. The wind had died to a whisper, leaving the gray sea rolling in long, glassy swells beneath a sky the color of tarnished pewter.

We're crossing the sixty-eighth parallel now, Mr. Pendelton, Captain Reed said, stepping out of the wheelhouse with a brass sextant tucked under his arm. Just passed twenty miles west of Jan Mayen's northern cape—though you wouldn't know it through this mist. Funny thing about the compass, though. Mr. Higgins at the wheel says the binnacle needle has been swinging three points east and west for the last half hour, even with the compensating magnets adjusted.

Arthur reached into his oilskin coat and drew out the brass astrolabe, holding it flat in his gloved palm. Just as Dr. Finch had predicted in Cambridge, the central silver-inlaid star had shifted slightly within its bezel, and when Arthur placed a small pocket compass beside the instrument, the needle snapped instantly toward the north-northwest, trembling with a faint, steady vibration.

Julian's bearings were exact, Arthur said quietly, looking out over the bow into the pale wall of sea fog. Keep her on this magnetic heading at half steam, Captain. According to the 1911 sounding log, the underwater basalt escarpment rises abruptly from twelve hundred fathoms within the next four miles.

Reed gave the order down the speaking tube to the engine room, and the steady thump of the auxiliary steam pistons slowed to a rhythmic, cautious pulse. Two crewmen stationed themselves in the bow chains with heavy lead lines, calling out the depth every two minutes as the schooner glided silently through the mist.

No bottom at two hundred fathoms! the leadsman sang out, his voice carrying clearly across the still water.

Minutes dragged by in tense silence. Arthur stood at the bow rail beside the first mate, squinting into the milky fog ahead, while the brass astrolabe grew noticeably warm against his palm through the wool of his glove.

Bottom at sixty fathoms—hard basalt! the leadsman shouted suddenly, hauling his line in rapidly. Forty fathoms! Twenty-five fathoms!

Hard astern! Reed bellowed from the quarterdeck, spinning the engine-room telegraph himself.

The *Endurance* shuddered as her propeller reversed, churning the glassy gray water into white foam, and glided to a gentle halt less than a cable's length from a dark, towering wall of columnar basalt that loomed out of the mist like the battlements of a sunken fortress.

Arthur stared upward in awe. Rising nearly eighty feet sheer out of the freezing sea was a crescent of jagged black volcanic rock, crowned with patches of ancient blue glacial ice. Directly ahead, where two massive pillars of basalt leaned inward toward one another, lay a narrow, deep-water channel no wider than sixty feet, leading into a sheltered, circular lagoon entirely hidden from the open ocean.

The First Anchorage, Arthur breathed, unfolding Julian's vellum chart and comparing the twin basalt pillars against the ink sketch penned in 1911. It is all here—the exact hexagonal basin, the sheltered entrance, everything the 1504 pilot described.

With two crewmen stationed at the bow with long wooden fenders and the steam engine turning over at its lowest speed, Captain Reed guided the *Endurance* inch by inch through the narrow basalt gap. As the schooner cleared the entrance pillars and entered the circular basin beyond, the heavy sea mist thinned abruptly, revealing a sight that brought every man on deck to a stunned, breathless standstill.

The basin was a near-perfect hexagon roughly four hundred yards across, surrounded on all sides by sheer cliffs of dark basalt that shielded the water from the ocean swells. At the northern end of the lagoon, resting on a broad shelf of black stone ten feet above the waterline, stood a circular tower built of seamless, dark gray masonry blocks fitted together without a trace of mortar. The tower was perhaps sixty feet high, tapering slightly toward a flat parapet, and from an open lantern gallery at its summit rose a steady, smokeless column of pale blue-white light—cold, silent, and brilliant enough to cast sharp shadows across the dark water even in the gray Arctic daylight.

The stone tower that burns without fuel, Captain Reed muttered, slowly removing his peaked cap as he stared across the water. God above, Mr. Pendelton—your uncle wasn't mad after all.

Within twenty minutes, the *Endurance* had dropped anchor in fifteen fathoms of clear, black water fifty yards off the stone shelf, and Arthur, Captain Reed, and two armed seamen rowed ashore in the ship's dory, carrying lanterns, measuring instruments, and a heavy pry bar.

When Arthur stepped out of the dory onto the smooth, wet basalt landing, he felt a faint, steady hum vibrating up through the soles of his boots—the same subtle resonance he had felt in the brass astrolabe. Broad steps cut directly into the living rock led up toward the base of the tower, where a tall, arched portal stood recessed behind two fluted stone columns engraved with the exact same geometric star pattern inlaid into the central key of the astrolabe.

Arthur approached the portal slowly, heart hammering against his ribs. Set into the center of the seamless dark metal door at waist height was a circular hollow surrounded by three raised bronze pins.

Without hesitation, Arthur drew the brass astrolabe from his coat, pressed the release catch, and removed the assembled silver-inlaid disk from its center. Holding his breath, he placed the disk into the circular recess on the door.

The three internal pins engaged with a deep, resonant chime that echoed across the stone basin. The geometric star on the disk flared briefly with the same pale blue luminescence burning atop the tower, and with a low, effortless grind of counterweights concealed deep within the basalt cliff, the massive metal door slid smoothly sideways into the wall.

Beyond the threshold lay a vast, vaulted chamber carved straight into the heart of the mountain, illuminated by narrow strips of the same cold blue light running along the upper cornices. Lining both walls were towering racks of dark metal cylinders and sealed crystal tablets arranged in meticulous rows, while in the center of the hall stood a raised circular table bearing a three-dimensional relief map of the entire globe—showing coastlines, landmasses, and ocean basins as they had existed before the last great rise of the seas.

Before Arthur could take more than three steps inside the threshold, the sharp crack of a rifle shot echoed across the lagoon outside, followed immediately by the frantic clanging of the *Endurance's* ship's bell.

Arthur spun around and sprinted back out onto the stone landing beside Captain Reed. Emerging through the narrow basalt entrance channel from the open sea was a sleek, steel-hulled steam yacht flying no flag, its bow already swinging broadside to bring a deck-mounted gun to bear on the anchored schooner. Standing on the yacht's forward bridge with a pair of binoculars raised to his eyes was Gareth Sterling.

They followed our coal smoke across the strait, Reed growled, drawing his revolver as he ran back toward the dory. Get back into that vault, Mr. Pendelton, and seal that door behind you! My crew and I will hold them off from the schooner—they won't take the *Endurance* without a fight!

No! Arthur shouted, stopping at the water's edge as another rifle shot splintered the gunwale of the dory. If they shell the schooner in this narrow basin, you won't have room to maneuver! Look at the entrance channel—it is barely sixty feet wide!

Arthur looked back up at the towering stone lantern gallery burning silently above them, then down at the brass astrolabe still clutched tightly in his left hand. Whoever had built this outpost had not left it defenseless against intruders who entered the lagoon without permission.

With a sudden surge of clarity, Arthur raced back up the carved basalt steps into the vaulted hall, heading straight for the central circular table beneath the relief map. Just as he had suspected, set into the rim of the stone table directly facing the entrance of the basin was a second circular recess, flanked by three small levers engraved with directional glyphs matching the outer rings of the astrolabe.

Outside, the deep boom of the yacht's deck gun shook the cliffs, sending a shower of loose pebbles rattling down into the water as a shell exploded harmlessly against the basalt wall twenty yards astern of the *Endurance*.

Arthur pressed the silver-inlaid disk into the recess on the central table and pulled the outer bronze lever sharply toward himself.

Deep beneath the basalt floor, a profound, rhythmic thrum awakened, rising rapidly in pitch until the very air in the chamber vibrated. High above on the tower parapet, the pale blue flame intensified tenfold, casting a blinding beam downward into the dark water of the lagoon.

Across the basin, beneath the narrow entrance channel where Sterling's steel yacht was maneuvering to fire a second round, the black water suddenly boiled upward in a violent vortex of white foam. Two massive, submerged pylons of dark alloy rose smoothly from the seabed directly beneath the yacht's keel, lifting the steel hull halfway out of the water and wedging it fast across the narrow basalt entrance like a cork in a bottle.

The yacht's steam whistle shrieked wildly as her engines churned uselessly in the air, trapped immovable between the twin pillars of the ancient harbor barrier.

For several seconds, silence fell over the lagoon save for the hiss of escaping steam from the stranded yacht and the gentle lap of waves against the stone steps. Slowly, Arthur lifted the silver disk from the table, walked back out into the cool Arctic air, and stood beside Captain Reed on the landing as the *Endurance's* crew lowered two longboats to row across and secure the surrendered vessel.

Looking out over the quiet hexagonal basin, with the timeless blue beacon shining steadily above the ancient vaults and Julian's life's work finally vindicated, Arthur Pendelton realized that his journey had not been an escape from the past after all. It had been a crossing over a threshold—and the real work of understanding what lay inside that mountain had only just begun.
